What [large language model][wp-llm] (LLM)-based AI produces is, in and of itself, an interpretation-opaque output.
I use this term rather than simply saying LLMs because the human user's messages and [tool call][openai-function-calling] results matter immensely for the epistemic grounding of AI's responses, as they supply dynamic inputs from the real world.

## Elaboration
An issue I take with certain practical [tips & tricks][pavel-ru-prompt] about AI or prompt engineering is that they're often lists of unrelated advice without any justification as to why I should trust this advice.
As some academic researcher, I'm more accustomed to works that formulate a starting thesis and then develop the line of thought methodically from it.

I think, tentatively, the elephant in the room from which I'd attempt to derive all practical advice is that, like with some (other) advanced machine learning models, one doesn't really get to trace an LLM-based AI's output causally, i.e., to tell which claim came from which premise.
Rather, people end up trusting the process (i.e., how the model was trained) and any robustness checks applied to the generated outputs.

With LLMs, the situation of course is complicated by the fact that the output is not mere numbers but coherent natural text.
(While LLM-based AI is not the same as a plain LLM as stated above, the final output of an agentic turn is still LLM-generated.)
I think a responsible user of AI needs to teach themself to combat this illusion and appreciate one simple fact in 2026: Natural language is no longer humans' alone but is also shared with machines.
Natural language is now being used (by humans and computers alike) to write computer inputs and by a computer to produce outputs.
This notion truly is as simple as that.
AI users need to acknowledge and deeply appreciate this new use of natural language as a computer programming language and also a kind of computer output.

From this, a lot of things follow.

### Inputs to AI 
The need for [context engineering][tweet-karpathy-context-engineering] is obviously one thing – if it's a programming language, we got to learn how to use it efficiently. Unfortunately because it's not a human-made language but something that has evolved naturally over the humanity's history (and continues evolving every day), it's not something we have a manual for. It's thus a programming language that the humanity needs to study as a natural phenomenon to become proficient in using it. As we noted, it's a new use of natural language, and it's accordingly poorly studied. In evidence-based medicine lingo, I guess something akin to "very low" [certainty of evidence][gradepro-coe] may be noted, and thus students should expect prompt engineering guidance to evolve significantly over time.

### AI outputs
The need for output decoding is also very important. Here, I think we won't be wrong if we say that the certainty of evidence also feels "very low" and subject to substantial change over short periods of time. Here what matters is that the output is important because LLMs are powerful.
(Of note, calling it [bullshit][mizrahi-no-2025] or [stochastic parroting][bender-dangers-2021] sounds like a non-serious joke to me, in 2026, when AI has been [confirmed][lean-fro-mathlib-2026-11-23] to [have been used][alpoge-remark-2026] to solve [Millenium][clay-millenium] math problems; so it's not a conjecture anymore but rather is a fact we need to deal with).
However, it's not clear what parts of it can be trusted and to what degree. As mentioned, the output is opaque to interpretation by default. An efficient tactic today is to produce a _secondary_ output using a deterministic, established method that verifies or proves some aspect of the AI output. This could be a hand-run web search, a Lean compilation, a sanity check against one's knowledge, or something else, the point being that an AI output, as is, is quite dangerous to be taken at face value, and the user is much safer developing the habit of deriving a verifiable/traditional artifact first before taking up any learnings downstream.

## Miscellanea
Below are some other ways to re-express the core idea everyone needs to know about AI.

### AI is not your friend
AI is a machine that follows instructions, i.e., a computer.
Or, it's just a [slop generator][pavel-ai-slop], depending on one's perspective.

### AI is an instance of alien cognition
Not a [huge deal][pavel-ai-afraid] perhaps, though – haven't we all watched _Star Wars_?

### AI outputs as raw research data
When [OpenAI announced][openai-2026-10-26] they had proved the Exponential Time Hypothesis is false, they called AI-generated manuscripts "a form of raw research data" that ultimately needs to be converted into "scientific understanding."
This aligns nicely with the [elaboration](#elaboration) above.

### Llmogram
LLMs provide us with a very interesting picture of reality – the reality as encoded in language patterns.
Language itself, including images and audio, is being used to paint the picture of reality – an ["LLM-o-gram,"][pavel-ai-llmogram] just like we would use a paint brush or a spectrogram.

### Exploratory vs. explanatory use of AI
Random fact: Creswell, Plano Clark et al.'s pragmatic typology of [mixed-methods][obssr-mixed-methods] research designs features two sequential designs: explanatory sequential and exploratory sequential.
Though unrelated, these map nicely onto "responsible uses of AI" – as follows, with examples:

**Explanatory (sequential):**
The go-to approach for Q&A when you already know where to look for the answer (i.e., without AI). For example, if you know what specific resource contains the answer to your question, go and look up in the source. Don't use AI for this, as you would be introducing the downsides of AI absolutely unnecessarily. Then – if necessary! – go to AI and ask it to explain (or elaborate on) what you found. Hence "explanatory." This is similar to how this mixed-methods research design works: quantitative ("hard science") first, qualitative ("soft science") next to explain the quantitative.

**Exploratory (sequential):**
When you don't know where to look for the answer, you just need _an_ answer. For example, some obscure problem you never ever heard about – and are not even sure what guide to look up. You go to AI. It produces some exploratory output, which is exploratory because you go and read about it following the AI's lead – in authoritative sources this time. This is similar to how this mixed-methods research design works when "soft" qualitative data are being used as a seed to then conduct a "hard science" quantitative study.

It's kind of interesting how mixed-methods ideologists' pragmatic view of science fits here. Qualitative is considered "thick" – narrow but deep. Quantitative is considered "thin" – wide but shallow. So what's underpinning the mixed-methods ideology is always this belief that for each research question, there's one best fitting research method.
Personally I [don't agree][pavel-b-realism] with this.
However, a pragmatic typology can suddenly come handy if one is asked to give tips & tricks (as in: prompt engineering advice).
By suggesting this mapping, therefore, we're assuming that AI is roughly akin to qualitative research (opaque, unverifiable directly but potentially very helpful) while human ways are roughly akin to quant (verifiable, objective, auditable). 

Here's to humans somehow turning from subjectivity into the reference for objectivity!
While AI, in its turn, becomes meta-subjective. That is to say, while we're holding human ways as the reference, regardless of subjective or objective ways (for example, human expert opinion is subjective – but we still hold it as ground truth versus AI), AI takes the place of the [subjective counterpart][pavel-ai-trust].

### Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis
It is a so-called Lean proof, i.e., computer code in the Lean language, which can be run to establish a formal mathematical claim.
Lean proof is [the first good proof][lean-fro-mathlib-2026-11-23], and then mathematicians will work (some, surely, will also use AI closely) towards shorter and more interpretable proofs.
We're noting, though, that the scientific breakthrough is the fact of the Lean provers' acceptance of the code, not the code itself – which aligns nicely with my [elaboration](#elaboration) above.
